# Mobile game core loops, first-time user experience (FTUE) and early retention (D0 to D7)

Research date: 2026-10-22. Method: web search plus page fetches (about 17 tool calls). Confidence labels: HIGH = primary or directly stated by a reputable source and consistent across sources; MEDIUM = single reasonable source or vendor blog with a stated basis; LOW = unsourced figure, old source, or vendor claim that could not be verified.

Global caveat: most public benchmark material comes from analytics or ad-tech vendors and blogs. Genre-level retention numbers are the weakest data in this area (see question 3).

## 1. What is a core loop (core / meta / session loops) and what does a minimal viable one look like across genres?

### Takeaway
The core loop is the short action, reward, progression cycle the player repeats every few seconds to minutes; the meta layer wraps it with longer-term goals (upgrades, collections, map progress, events) that give a reason to come back for days or weeks. "Session loop" is not a standardized term: sources use "meta loop" for the session-to-session or long-term layer, and no source found defines a separate session loop. Hybrid-casual games are, by Voodoo's definition, a hyper-casual core plus a simplified casual/midcore meta layer.

### Cited Findings
- Core loop is "the very heartbeat" of the game: a chain of actions the player repeats as the main experience; it should be short, simple and fit the theme and rules. The same page lists variants: dual loop (the loop forks into two paths so the player can stop after the first loop or continue), nested loop, and compulsion loop (habit-forming loops rooted in psychology). It does not define "meta loop" or "session loop". (2023-03-28) — [Homa Games, What is a core loop in a mobile game](https://www.homagames.com/blog/what-is-a-core-loop-in-a-mobile-game). Confidence: HIGH for the core loop definition, MEDIUM as a general taxonomy (vendor blog).
- Hyper-casual example loop: play, earn diamonds, replay to unlock new themes (Stack by Ketchapp). Clash of Clans-style loop: collect coins and elixir, build the settlement, expand troops, battle for more resources. Generic arcade idle / city builder loop: earn coins, craft items, grow the town. (2023-03-28) — [Homa Games](https://www.homagames.com/blog/what-is-a-core-loop-in-a-mobile-game). Confidence: MEDIUM.
- Search summaries of several loop-explainer pages describe the core loop as the minute-to-minute fun and the metagame as the reason to come back for weeks and months; one source calls the meta loop "session-to-session", others call it long-term progression. Terminology is inconsistent between authors. — [GDevelop, Casual Game Loops Explained](https://gdevelop.io/blog/casual-game-loops) and [MWM glossary: core loop](https://mwm.ai/glossary/core-loop) (found in search results; I did not open the full pages). Confidence: LOW to MEDIUM (search snippets only).
- Hybrid-casual definition: combines core hyper-casual gameplay with simplified meta-gameplay borrowed from casual or midcore games; the meta features add medium and long-term goals. Voodoo reported uplift in D7 and D14 retention in its tests without publishing exact numbers (per search summary; the interview page itself returned HTTP 403 when fetched). — [PocketGamer.biz, Corentin Selz (Voodoo): from hypercasual to hybrid casual](https://www.pocketgamer.biz/interview/77247/corentin-selz-voodoo-from-hypercasual-to-hybrid-casual/). Confidence: MEDIUM (secondhand snippet, page not read).
- One guide frames the meta layer as the retention engine and says that by D3-D5 the meta should be the main reason a player relaunches the app; examples: collections, map progression, seasonal events, battle passes. Hyper-casual is judged mostly on D1 and D3, hybrid models add D7, D30 and LTV. — [Game Growth Advisor, hybrid-casual design strategy 2026](https://gamegrowthadvisor.com/blog/2026-04-16-hybrid-casual-game-design-strategy-2026/) (search snippet, 2026-04-16). Confidence: MEDIUM.
- Voodoo uses 55% D1 and 22% D7 as minimum viable prototype thresholds for hybrid-casual testing (per a 2026 vendor analysis, not read at the primary source). — [Game Growth Advisor, mobile game KPIs benchmarks 2026](https://gamegrowthadvisor.com/blog/2026-03-17-mobile-game-kpis-benchmarks-2026/) / search summary. Confidence: LOW to MEDIUM (secondhand).
- Idle/incremental: a developer postmortem reports the first prestige (reset-for-bonus meta step) took 25-35 minutes for most players and the developer would now aim for 15-20; players who bounce before then never see the real game. An AdVenture Capitalist postmortem advises not unveiling every system at once and using goal-setting so players drive toward unlocking the next system. — [DEV Community, Doomscroll 2077 economy](https://dev.to/tanishpaul1106/aura-data-and-prestige-designing-the-economy-of-doomscroll-2077-293f); [Global Games Forum, AdVenture Capitalist lessons](https://www.globalgamesforum.com/ctv/idle-game-design-lessons-from-developing-adventure-capitalist). Confidence: LOW to MEDIUM (single developer blog; AdVenture Capitalist from search snippet).
- Roblox's onboarding docs tell designers to teach the core loop explicitly: the repeated actions that drive progress, and both what to do and why. — [Roblox Creator Docs, Onboarding](https://create.roblox.com/docs/production/game-design/onboarding) (undated, fetched 2026-10). Confidence: HIGH that this is the guidance; MEDIUM that it generalizes to non-Roblox games.

### Inferences
- A minimal viable stack that fits all sources: (1) core action with instant feedback, (2) reward every loop, (3) a visible next goal outside the loop (upgrade, level, unlock, prestige), (4) a reason to return later (timer, event, daily reward). Hyper-casual has only 1-2; hybrid-casual adds 3; midcore (RPG/4X) adds 4 and heavy meta. This is my synthesis, not a quoted taxonomy.
- Genre minimum loops (inferred from the examples above plus general knowledge, not individually sourced): match-3 = match, clear level, earn boosters or progress on a map; idle = tap/auto-earn, buy upgrade, prestige; RPG/gacha = battle, earn resources, upgrade or summon heroes, push stages; 4X = collect, build, train, fight, repeat on timers; casual sim = produce, sell, expand, decorate.

### Gaps
- No authoritative source found that defines "session loop" distinctly from core or meta loop. Treat as an unresolved terminology contradiction between sources.
- Genre-specific minimum loop descriptions for match-3, RPG/gacha, 4X and casual sim were not found in citable sources (searches returned player guides and store listings). A GDC talk or Deconstructor of Fun teardown for each genre is the next step.
- The Voodoo interview and the Game Growth Advisor hybrid-casual page were seen only through search summaries.

## 2. What does a good FTUE look like (time to first fun, tutorial length, guided first wins, scripted rewards, when to ask for login / push / rating)?

### Takeaway
Consensus advice: put players into the fun within about 2-3 minutes, teach by doing in small steps, give a guaranteed early success plus a reward, and defer login, ads, store offers and permission prompts until the player has felt the fun. No source gives a hard numeric standard for tutorial length; the rule is "as short as the genre allows", with well-known genres (match-3, city builders) able to skip straight into play.

### Cited Findings
- Show why the game is fun within "two to three minutes"; the whole game need not be explained by then. Let players head straight into play for well-understood genres such as city builders or match-3. (Page last updated 2025-02-26) — [GameAnalytics, 10 tips for a great FTUE in F2P games](https://gameanalytics.com/blog/tips-for-a-great-first-time-user-experience-ftue-in-f2p-games). Confidence: HIGH that this is the stated advice; the 2-3 minute figure is unsupported by data (MEDIUM).
- Same source: avoid hitting new users at startup with a paywall, an ad, or a requirement to create an account on an obscure platform; let the tutorial give a form of success; give premium currency that buys a meaningful upgrade (also teaches how to buy later); limit purchase options early; teach customization early because attachment lifts retention; give goals once the open-ended game begins. It gives no timing guidance for login, push or rating prompts. (2025-02-26) — [GameAnalytics](https://gameanalytics.com/blog/tips-for-a-great-first-time-user-experience-ftue-in-f2p-games). Confidence: MEDIUM.
- Track every tutorial step as a funnel; find the largest drop-offs; compare first-time payer timing; check compulsion-loop sources and sinks. — [GameAnalytics](https://gameanalytics.com/blog/tips-for-a-great-first-time-user-experience-ftue-in-f2p-games). Confidence: HIGH (method advice).
- Supersonic (Unity LevelPlay), 2025-03-03: no numeric tutorial length. Case: in the game Trash Tycoon, jumping too quickly between mechanics caused 60% of players to churn during the tutorial; giving players up to 1 minute of play with no instructions caused confusion and churn (no figure). Celebrate early wins with rewards and confetti; introduce currencies gradually (flooding lowers perceived value); ads can wait until players are hooked; small changes such as removing one tutorial step or one click can meaningfully change retention; keep the tutorial identical for everyone so the data stays clean. — [Supersonic, Nailing the first impression: 7 ways to optimize your FTUE](https://supersonic.com/learn/blog/optimizing-ftue/). Confidence: MEDIUM (vendor blog, one case).
- Roblox docs: players "typically decide their interest in a game within minutes"; keep early XP thresholds low so players level up quickly; give starter items and currency (A/B test amounts); end onboarding with clear short-, mid- and long-term goals and a deliberate "moment of joy"; define D1 retention as the share getting through the FTUE funnel. No numeric time targets. — [Roblox Creator Docs, Onboarding](https://create.roblox.com/docs/production/game-design/onboarding). Confidence: MEDIUM (platform-specific, undated).
- Unity Learn course: give a reward during the FTUE to encourage consecutive sessions and show rewards for the next week or month; layered quests (daily to multi-day); plan retention tracking early. Page updated 2026-08-04. — [Unity Learn, Build in retention and player engagement](https://learn.unity.com/tutorial/how-to-build-in-retention-and-player-engagement-as-part-of-your-early-concept). Confidence: MEDIUM. (A search snippet claimed this page uses a six-level puzzle tutorial example; the fetched page did not contain it, so that claim is dropped.)
- Push permission: do not ask on first launch; ask contextually after a pre-permission screen that explains the benefit; if the soft prompt is declined, ask again in a later session; for asynchronous multiplayer ask early (turn alerts), for timer games ask the first time a player starts a timer on a valuable resource. The article cites an average opt-in rate of about 33% in 2014. (2015-07-14, old) — [Adrian Crook, Timers, opt-ins and chimes](https://adriancrook.com/?p=4922). Confidence: MEDIUM on the logic, LOW on the number (2014 data).
- iOS shows the system notification dialog once per install, which is why vendors recommend a soft ask first; Apple's provisional authorization delivers quietly without a prompt. Vendor blogs report 2-3x opt-in uplift from priming, unverified; one vendor reports 75% of push-sending games asked immediately on launch. — [Iterable](https://iterable.com/blog/when-to-request-permission-to-send-push-notifications/), [OneSignal docs](https://documentation.onesignal.com/docs/en/prompt-for-push-permissions) (search summaries). Confidence: MEDIUM for the one-shot mechanic, LOW for the uplift figures.
- Rating prompt: iOS StoreKit shows the system prompt at most three times per 365 days per user; Apple's guidance is to wait at least a week or two between requests and prompt again only after more engagement; developer blogs say not to call it in response to a button tap; blogs cite guideline 1.1.7 banning incentivized reviews (not verified against Apple's text). — [Apple Developer Forums thread quoting Apple guidance](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/115193) and [Atomic Object](https://spin.atomicobject.com/ios-review-prompt-customize/) (search summaries, undated). Confidence: MEDIUM.
- Segwise (2025-02-07, updated 2026) lists uncited tactics: interactive tutorials (Clash Royale as the example), Day-1 reward, reminder push when a player quits mid-tutorial, delay permission and sign-in requests until after the player has felt the fun, and a StriveCloud-cited claim that good onboarding lifts retention up to 50% (no date, not verified). — [Segwise](https://segwise.ai/blog/mobile-gaming-app-user-retention-strategies.md). Confidence: LOW.
- Over-restrictive onboarding can inflate D1 while D7 collapses because players are unprepared for the live game. — [iGaming Business via search summary](https://igamingbusiness.com/?p=9760). Confidence: LOW (snippet only, tangential source).

### Inferences
- The recurring structure is: instant playable moment, forced-success first level(s), tutorial reward, then delayed asks (login, push, rating, offers). Every source agrees on "delay asks" but none gives a measured optimal delay, so exact timing is a test-it-yourself variable.
- Login can usually be anonymous/guest first and linked later (consistent with "no forced account creation" above); I did not find a source quantifying the retention cost of forced login.

### Gaps
- No 2023-2026 primary data on time-to-first-fun, tutorial length versus retention, or tutorial completion rates by genre. GameAnalytics' benchmark report does not cover FTUE.
- No citable source on optimal timing of login prompts or rating prompts in games specifically (rating rules are from Apple guidance via secondary pages; the Apple documentation itself was not fetched).
- Gacha/RPG-specific FTUE (guaranteed first pull, scripted summon) found no sources.
- Match-3 tutorial sequencing (how fast boosters and lives are introduced) found no design sources, only player guides.
- Several citations are search-result summaries rather than fully read pages; this is flagged per item.

## 3. Typical D1, D7, D30 retention benchmarks and FTUE drop-off by genre; "good" versus "great"

All-genre mobile medians (GameAnalytics, 2025 data, published in the 2026 report) are roughly D1 22%, D7 under 4%, D30 about 0.7%; top 10% reaches about 40% / 11-12% / about 4%. Genre-level numbers are inconsistent and mostly unreliable: the sources conflict by large factors (see contradictions), so the report should present per-genre numbers only as directional, and prefer the all-genre percentile distribution.

### Cited Findings
- GameAnalytics mobile, 16,262 games with at least 1,000 MAU, 2025 data (2026 report): D1 top 25% just above 30%, top 10% about 40%, top 1% 64-68%; D7 median just under 4%, top 25% 6-7%, top 10% 11-12%, top 1% above 25% (sometimes about 28%); D30 median 0.69-0.79%, top 25% about 1.6-1.8%, top 1% 13-15%. D1 declined in 2025 versus 2024. Report gives aggregated figures across all genres, no genre split. — [GameDevReports summary of GameAnalytics Mobile and PC Benchmarks 2026](https://gamedevreports.substack.com/p/gameanalytics-mobile-and-pc-game) (2026). Confidence: HIGH for the numbers as reported by the summary; the primary report PDF was not opened. One search result noted the summary post is sponsored (PWN Games); flag for the writer.
- Median D1 about 22%, D7 3.4-3.9%, D30 0.68-0.79%; top quartile D1 25-27% Android and 31-33% iOS; D7 7-8%; sources are GameAnalytics 2025 (11,600 games, 1.48B MAU) and the 2026 cut. (2026-03-17, updated 2026-08-01) — [Game Growth Advisor, Mobile game KPIs benchmarks 2026](https://gamegrowthadvisor.com/blog/2026-03-17-mobile-game-kpis-benchmarks-2026/). Confidence: HIGH (consistent with the line above).
- Same article on genre: casual and puzzle on Android D1 28-32%, D7 9-12%, D30 3.5-5%, from AppsFlyer Q3 2022 data (apps with attribution SDK, skewed toward paid-acquisition studios); the author says the last broadly published genre breakdown is AppsFlyer's Q3 2022 and removed an earlier genre grid because no verifiable genre-level retention exists beyond casual/puzzle on Android. — [Game Growth Advisor](https://gamegrowthadvisor.com/blog/2026-03-17-mobile-game-kpis-benchmarks-2026/). Confidence: LOW.

Top reasons players download and engage: gameplay quality (67%). Return drivers: engagement bonuses (64%), in-game events (50%), frequent content updates (34%). — [GameDevReports summary](https://gamedevreports.substack.com/p/mistplay-and-appsflyer-player-loyalty). Confidence: MEDIUM.
- MWM dataset (US, Q3 2025) for the whole "Game" app category: median D1 36.6%, D7 9.3%, D30 2.9% — different population (apps in MWM's catalog), not comparable to GameAnalytics. — [MWM glossary: retention](https://mwm.ai/glossary/retention) (search summary). Confidence: LOW to MEDIUM.

Confidence: HIGH as reported.

### Contradictions to preserve
- RPG D30: Segwise/Mistplay-attributed 3.48% versus Playio 15-25%. Simulation D30: 2.96% versus 20-30%. Strategy D1: 25.39% versus 35-50%. The Playio ranges exceed GameAnalytics' top-1% D30 (13-15%), so they cannot be general market figures; they are probably targets for scaled, UA-driven titles. Unresolved.
- Hyper-casual D7: 5.90% (Segwise) vs 5-10% (Playio) are roughly consistent; hybrid-casual D7 15-22% (a vendor blog via search summary) vs a Tier 1 viability threshold of about 18% D7 (Game Growth Advisor) are roughly consistent but both vendor claims.
- A search summary of a Juego Studio table gives D1 of 45-60% for simulation; Segwise gives 30.10%. Same pattern: unsourced table versus a table attributed to Mistplay.

### Inferences
- Reading the distributions: median is a mediocre game, the top 10% (D1 about 40%, D7 about 11-12%, D30 about 4%) is a strong game, the top 1% (D1 64-68%, D7 25%+, D30 13-15%) is exceptional. "Good" for a commercially scaled title is therefore likely top quartile or better; the vendor "good" tiers (D1 35-40, D7 18-22) sit around top 10% to top 1% of the all-game distribution, which fits the idea that they describe UA-driven titles.
- Retention is steep: median D1 to D7 loses about 80%, D7 to D30 about 80%. Calculated from the median figures above (22% to under 4% to about 0.7%).

### Gaps
- No trustworthy 2023-2026 genre-level D1/D7/D30 table. The Mistplay original, AppsFlyer's current genre report, Adjust's and Liftoff's 2025-2026 reports were not retrieved. Treat genre numbers as unreliable.
- No source for FTUE-stage drop-off percentages by genre (for example tutorial step 1 to tutorial complete).
- GameAnalytics primary PDF (investgame.net link in search results) not opened; figures come from a Substack summary.
- Casual sim, idle, and 4X specific benchmarks: none found that survive the quality bar.

## 4. Which early-game patterns recur, and what is the designer intent behind each?

### Takeaway
Recurring patterns are: fast early progression (low XP thresholds, quick level-ups), staged feature unlocks, scripted early gifts of soft and premium currency, tutorial completion rewards, a celebratory "moment of joy", early daily-reward streaks, and a first-purchase offer. Sources state the intent as: prove value quickly, avoid overload, teach the economy, create a habit, and create attachment. Few sources link a pattern to measured D1/D7 effects.

### Cited Findings
- Fast early progression: keep early XP thresholds low so players level up quickly (intent: show value through progression within the first minutes). — [Roblox Creator Docs, Onboarding](https://create.roblox.com/docs/production/game-design/onboarding). Confidence: MEDIUM.
- Early levels should feel fun and achievable, with mild friction added gradually (intent: avoid early failure while building mastery). — [Supersonic](https://supersonic.com/learn/blog/optimizing-ftue/) (2025-03-03). Confidence: MEDIUM.
- Gradual feature unlocks and one mechanic at a time (intent: avoid overload, give each new feature focus). A vendor-cited claim says apps presenting more than five features in onboarding saw 30% higher early drop-out, with unknown origin. — [Adrian Crook, Best practices for mobile game onboarding](https://adriancrook.com/?p=8055) (search summary, undated); the 30% figure from [lifescapes.org](https://www.lifescapes.org/362303.html) is unverifiable. Confidence: MEDIUM for the practice, LOW for the 30% number.
- Idle/AdVenture Capitalist: unlock systems gradually and tie them to visible goals so players intentionally drive toward the next system. — [Global Games Forum](https://www.globalgamesforum.com/ctv/idle-game-design-lessons-from-developing-adventure-capitalist). Confidence: LOW to MEDIUM.
- Early currency gifts: a premium-currency gift that buys a significant upgrade both rewards the player and teaches them how to make a later purchase (intent: teach the economy and the purchase flow). — [GameAnalytics](https://gameanalytics.com/blog/tips-for-a-great-first-time-user-experience-ftue-in-f2p-games) (2025-02-26). Confidence: MEDIUM.
- Per-step tutorial rewards: small currency payouts per tutorial step hook players and introduce the economy (Udonis); a completion reward such as an exclusive item or soft currency gives a sense of accomplishment (Beamable). — [Udonis](https://www.blog.udonis.co/mobile-marketing/mobile-games/first-time-user-experience), [Beamable](https://beamable.com/blog/a-practical-guide-to-improving-player-retention) (search summaries). Confidence: LOW to MEDIUM.
- Currency scarcity moments: introduce currencies gradually and create moments where the player runs low, to encourage continued play or purchase (intent: give currency meaning). — [Supersonic](https://supersonic.com/learn/blog/optimizing-ftue/) (2025-03-03). Confidence: MEDIUM.
- Celebration and personalization: confetti and rewards on first wins; teach avatar/base customization early because emotional attachment raises retention. — [Supersonic](https://supersonic.com/learn/blog/optimizing-ftue/), [GameAnalytics](https://gameanalytics.com/blog/tips-for-a-great-first-time-user-experience-ftue-in-f2p-games). Confidence: MEDIUM.
- Early daily reward: reward during the FTUE to encourage consecutive sessions and preview rewards for the next week or month (intent: build the return habit). Engagement bonuses are the top self-reported return driver among surveyed US players (64%). — [Unity Learn](https://learn.unity.com/tutorial/how-to-build-in-retention-and-player-engagement-as-part-of-your-early-concept) (updated 2026-08-04); [GameDevReports on Mistplay and AppsFlyer](https://gamedevreports.substack.com/p/mistplay-and-appsflyer-player-loyalty) (2025-05-27; survey, self-reported). Confidence: MEDIUM.
- First-purchase offer: GameAnalytics tells designers to track first-time payers (how early they convert, at which point in play), and to limit purchase options early. No source found stating a specific first-purchase-offer design or measured effect. — [GameAnalytics](https://gameanalytics.com/blog/tips-for-a-great-first-time-user-experience-ftue-in-f2p-games). Confidence: MEDIUM that this is the advice; the offer pattern itself is a gap.
- Long-term goals at the end of onboarding: clear short-, mid- and long-term goals (intent: prevent the "now what?" gap after the scripted section). — [Roblox Creator Docs](https://create.roblox.com/docs/production/game-design/onboarding), [GameAnalytics](https://gameanalytics.com/blog/tips-for-a-great-first-time-user-experience-ftue-in-f2p-games). Confidence: MEDIUM.
- Unlock-timing research: a vendor page asks how early hero-unlock timing in the first three sessions shifts D14 retention but shows no results. — [Bruin](https://getbruin.com/use-cases/mobile-gaming/hero-unlock-retention-impact/). Confidence: LOW (no evidence).

### Inferences
- The patterns map to a single arc: minute 0-3 prove fun (fast progression, scripted success), minute 3-15 teach systems one at a time with gifts to seed the economy, session end set a goal and a reason to return (daily reward, timer, push soft-ask). This is my synthesis from the sources.
- The designer intent is consistently stated as "reduce early quitting" rather than "maximize long-term retention"; the iGaming Business caution (inflated D1, weak D7) suggests the two goals can conflict. Evidence is thin.

### Gaps
- No Deconstructor of Fun, Naavik or GameRefinery article on early-game patterns was retrieved; searches for them returned unrelated results. Mark as a research gap, not as absence of the literature.
- No measured effect sizes for any individual pattern (early gift, forced win, first-purchase offer, early unlocks).
- Genre specifics (gacha first-session summon, 4X protection shield and fast first builds, match-3 early level ease) not sourced.

## 5. Most common reasons players churn in the first session and first week

### Takeaway
Cited causes cluster in four groups: too slow or too long before fun (slow intro, long or patronizing tutorial), friction and hostility (forced account creation, early ads/paywalls, permission spam, bugs and crashes), difficulty mismatch (hard-to-learn controls, difficulty spikes, or trivial progression), and expectation mismatch between marketing and gameplay that surfaces in the first days. There is no rigorous quantified ranking.

### Cited Findings
- Sixteen reasons list (originally on the GameAnalytics blog, 2015-04-08): weak intro, sessions too long, wrong target audience, saturated segment, insufficient testing/bugs, slow tutorial, hard to pick up, sudden difficulty spikes, broken progression, grinding without intrinsic reward, high time investment before fun, toxic community, scarce resources (paywalled or slow progress), empty sessions, punishing inactivity, slow updates. — [Nathan Lovato, 16 reasons why players are leaving your game (Game Developer)](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/16-reasons-why-players-are-leaving-your-game). Confidence: HIGH that these are the author's claims; MEDIUM as current guidance (11 years old, opinion-based).
- Forcing experienced players through a long tutorial pushes them away; optional tutorials help. Withholding enjoyable content through long tutorials or slow starts loses players. — [Game Developer, 16 reasons](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/16-reasons-why-players-are-leaving-your-game) (2015). Confidence: MEDIUM.
- Jumping too quickly between mechanics: 60% churn in the tutorial in a single case. — [Supersonic](https://supersonic.com/learn/blog/optimizing-ftue/) (2025-03-03). Confidence: LOW to MEDIUM.
- Expectation mismatch: early days test whether the game matches what marketing promised, often surfacing around day 3. Survey data: gameplay quality is the top driver of downloads and engagement (67% of surveyed US players), and ad-to-gameplay accuracy matters more to Android users (nearly twice iOS). — [Scoop Analytics](https://www.scoopanalytics.com/blog/why-do-mobile-players-love-my-game-for-3-days-then-delete-it) (search summary, undated; claim is an argument, not data); [GameDevReports on Mistplay and AppsFlyer](https://gamedevreports.substack.com/p/mistplay-and-appsflyer-player-loyalty) (2025-05-27). Confidence: LOW for the day-3 claim, MEDIUM for the survey figures (self-reported).
- Technical problems: players who hit crashes or poor performance in the first session often quit without reporting why (argument, no data). — [Bugnet](https://bugnet.io/blog/how-do-i-find-out-why-players-quit-my-game) (search summary). Confidence: LOW.
- Aggressive ads or paywalls, excessive notifications, and difficulty spikes are listed as churn factors in vendor guides. — [Udonis churn rate](https://blog.udonis.co/mobile-marketing/mobile-games/churn-rate) (search summary). Confidence: LOW.
- Session-length claim: sessions longer than nine minutes correlate with 31% D1 versus 20% for shorter sessions, no dataset named. — [Adrian Crook, how to reduce drop-off during onboarding](https://adriancrook.com/?p=8151) (search summary of an SEO-style guide). Confidence: LOW; also confounded (engaged players play longer, so long sessions may be an effect rather than a cause).
- Unverified headline stats circulating in blogs: "about 73% of players abandon within 24 hours" and "70.5% leave after Day 1 / 96.79% by Day 30". No primary source; do not reuse. They are loosely consistent with GameAnalytics D1 median of about 22% (about 78% gone by day 1), which does come from real data. — [Udonis churn rate](https://blog.udonis.co/mobile-marketing/mobile-games/churn-rate) (search summary) vs [GameDevReports on GameAnalytics](https://gamedevreports.substack.com/p/gameanalytics-mobile-and-pc-game). Confidence: LOW for the blog figures, HIGH for the GameAnalytics median.
- Academic: an Aalborg University thesis on three F2P games' first minutes (26 participants) found flow emerges during onboarding in all three and is linked to desire to keep playing. — [Aalborg University project portal](https://projekter.aau.dk/flow-based-assessment-of-the-onboarding-phase-in-f2p-mobile-games-1750b414.html) (search summary; small sample). Confidence: LOW to MEDIUM.

### Inferences
- The most defensible, mutually reinforcing causes: (1) fun arrives late, (2) friction before play (accounts, ads, prompts, long tutorial), (3) bugs or difficulty problems in the first minutes, (4) the game differs from the ad. Cause 4 plausibly explains why D1 is high but D7 collapses for ad-driven installs; this is my inference, partly supported by the survey on ad accuracy.
- First-week churn after day 1 is more tied to missing meta goals, scarce resources, and no return reason (timers, events, daily reward). Supported by the "empty sessions", "scarce resources" and "punishing inactivity" items above plus the meta-layer-by-D3-D5 guidance.

### Gaps
- No quantified ranking of churn reasons from a rigorous source (for example uninstall surveys or Adjust/AppsFlyer uninstall data). The Interpret churned-player study was found but its findings were not available in the snippet.
- First-session versus first-week split of reasons is not measured anywhere I found.
- The best-quality "why players quit" content is from 2015; recent (2023-2026) equivalents were not located.
