## 1. Which behavioral frameworks explain engagement design (Hook Model, SDT, Octalysis, Fogg, flow, Bartle, Quantic Foundry)?

### Takeaway
The frameworks answer different questions. Fogg (B=MAP) and Hook (trigger, action, variable reward, investment) explain how a habit loop forms. SDT, flow, Bartle and Quantic Foundry explain why people enjoy playing and what kinds of players exist. Octalysis is a practitioner checklist of 8 drives, popular in design but not a peer-reviewed theory. Only SDT and flow have strong academic standing; Hook, Octalysis and Bartle are practitioner or descriptive models.

### Cited Findings
- Fogg Behavior Model: a behavior happens when Motivation, Ability and a Prompt converge at the same moment (B=MAP); if the behavior does not happen, at least one of the three is missing. — [behaviormodel.org](https://behaviormodel.org/); HIGH
- Hook Model (Nir Eyal, book *Hooked*, 2014): a four-phase loop of Trigger (external, then internal), Action (simplest behavior in anticipation of reward), Variable Reward (three types: tribe/social, hunt/resources or information, self/mastery) and Investment (time, data, effort, social capital that loads the next trigger). — [Substack summary](https://productandrew.substack.com/p/product-79), [dsebastien concept page](https://concepts.dsebastien.net/concept/hook-model/); MEDIUM (only secondary summaries read; the dopamine framing in these summaries is commentary, not Eyal's own wording)
- Self-Determination Theory applied to games (Ryan, Rigby, Przybylski, *Motivation and Emotion*, 2006): in four studies, perceived in-game autonomy, competence and relatedness each independently predicted enjoyment and future play; the study produced the PENS questionnaire. — [Rochester copy of the 2006 paper](https://www.rochester.edu/warner/lida/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/02bfe513dd59366750000000.pdf), [ScienceDaily 2006](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2006/12/061226134706.htm), [SDT site PENS page](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/player-experience-of-needs-satisfaction-pens/); HIGH for the 2006 finding (as summarized in search results; the paper body was not read line by line)
- SDT extension: Przybylski, Weinstein, Ryan and Rigby (*Cyberpsychology & Behavior*, 2009) hypothesized that high need satisfaction fosters "harmonious" passion for games and low need satisfaction fosters "obsessive" passion. — [Ewha repository record](https://pure.ewha.ac.kr/en/publications/having-to-versus-wanting-to-play-background-and-consequences-of-h/), [ACU record](https://acuresearchbank.acu.edu.au/item/89x90/having-to-versus-wanting-to-play-background-and-consequences-of-harmonious-versus-obsessive-engagement-in-video-games); MEDIUM (abstract-level; results not read)
- Octalysis (Yu-kai Chou, framework 2012, book *Actionable Gamification* 2014): 8 core drives: Epic Meaning and Calling; Development and Accomplishment; Empowerment of Creativity and Feedback; Ownership and Possession; Social Influence and Relatedness; Scarcity and Impatience; Unpredictability and Curiosity; Loss and Avoidance. The University of Zurich summary calls it a practitioner model and says Development and Accomplishment is the most used drive. — [Drimify summary](https://drimify.com/en/resources/yukai-chou-8-drives-octalysis-model), [UZH Quartz definition](https://www.gbl.uzh.ch/quartz/definitions/Octalysis-Framework); MEDIUM. The Octalysis Group's own claim of "over 4,000 academic citations" was not verified.
- Flow (Csikszentmihalyi): preconditions are challenge-skill balance, clear goals and immediate feedback (plus a sense of control). GameFlow (Sweetser and Wyeth, 2005) adapts this into eight game elements: concentration, challenge, player skill, control, clear goals, feedback, immersion, social interaction; social interaction is an addition, not an original flow dimension. — [GameFlow PDF (NTNU)](https://ntnu.no/wiki/download/attachments/227443360/GameFlow.pdf?api=v2), [Game Developer: Gameplay Flow](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/gameplay-flow-designing-for-player-immersion); MEDIUM
- Bartle's four types (Achievers/Diamonds, Explorers/Spades, Socialisers/Hearts, Killers/Clubs), from the April 1996 paper "Hearts, Clubs, Diamonds, Spades: Players Who Suit MUDs", itself an extension of a 1990 article, based on 1989-1990 discussion among players on a UK MUD. Bartle states the types are "stereotypical", he is not a trained psychologist, and he does not expect conventional rigor. — [Bartle's paper](https://mud.co.uk/richard/hcds.htm); HIGH
- Quantic Foundry (Nick Yee, Nic Ducheneaut): 12 motivations built by factor analysis of large survey data (400,000+ players in the 2019 GDC talk; a 2025 report covers 466,000+ players aged 13-64), grouped into six clusters: Action, Social, Mastery, Achievement, Immersion, Creativity. — [GDC Vault talk page](https://gdcvault.com/play/1025742/A-Deep-Dive-into-the), [AppFollow guide](https://appfollow.io/blog/understanding-player-behavior-motivations), [PocketGamer.biz](https://www.pocketgamer.biz/understanding-gamer-motivations/); MEDIUM (quanticfoundry.com returned HTTP 403 when fetched)
- Quantic Foundry observation on mobile: Nick Yee reportedly says mobile players often cannot name the games they were recruited from, unlike console/PC players, and have less vocabulary for their tastes. "Completion" is reported in the top three motivations across all genders and age groups (not split by platform). — [Player Driven Substack interview](https://playerdriven.substack.com/p/quantic-foundrys-nick-yee-on-gamer), [GamePro (German)](https://www.gamepro.de/artikel/warum-wir-spielen-studie-sieht-unterschiedliche-gruende-bei-maennern-und-frauen,3307054.html); LOW-MEDIUM

### Inferences
- Collection, completion and "finish the set" hooks map to Quantic Foundry's Completion motivation and to Octalysis Ownership; social hooks map to Bartle Socialisers and SDT relatedness. This is my mapping, not a source claim.
- SDT and the harmonious/obsessive distinction are the most defensible academic lens for "engaging vs. exploitative": the same mechanic can serve autonomy/competence (want to play) or compulsion (have to play). The 2009 paper's hypothesis is directly relevant, but its results were not read.
- Hook and Octalysis are best described to the reader as practitioner playbooks. Their prescriptions are not validated by controlled trials in games (no such evidence found).

### Gaps
- Primary text of *Hooked* and of Csikszentmihalyi's 1990 *Flow* were not read; Hook and flow descriptions rest on secondary summaries.
- No Quantic Foundry mobile-specific numeric breakdown (site blocked; full report is restricted).
- Results of the 2009 harmonious/obsessive passion study were not read.

## 2. Which psychological mechanisms sit under the recurring hooks, and how solid is the evidence?

### Takeaway
Some mechanisms have strong direct evidence (endowed progress, goal gradient, near-miss recruitment of reward circuitry, variable-ratio persistence). Others are popular in game-design talk but contested: loss aversion is disputed for small stakes, and the Zeigarnik "unfinished tasks are remembered better" effect failed to replicate in a 2025 meta-analysis (the urge to resume tasks did hold). Treat "it triggers dopamine" claims in marketing literature as unproven.

### Cited Findings
- Endowed progress: Nunes and Drèze, *Journal of Consumer Research* 32(4), 2006. A car-wash loyalty-card field experiment (300 cards): a card needing 8 stamps vs a card needing 10 with 2 pre-stamped (same 8 purchases required). Redemption over nine months was 34% for the pre-stamped card vs 19% for the blank one. A reason for the endowment strengthens the effect. — [Wharton Knowledge](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/podcast/knowledge-at-wharton-podcast/the-lowdown-on-customer-loyalty-programs-which-are-the-most-effective-and-why/), [Silicon Canals](https://siliconcanals.com/t-car-wash-loyalty-cards-endowed-progress/); MEDIUM (figures from secondary summaries; paper itself not accessed)
- Goal gradient: Kivetz, Urminsky and Zheng, *Journal of Marketing Research* 43(1), 2006, 39-58. Café "buy 10 get 1 free" customers bought faster as they neared the reward; a 12-stamp card with 2 bonus stamps was completed faster than a plain 10-stamp card; stronger acceleration predicted retention. — [Columbia Business School page](https://business.columbia.edu/faculty/research/goal-gradient-hypothesis-resurrected-purchase-acceleration-illusionary-goal), [paper PDF](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/pubfiles/1200/goalgradient.pdf); HIGH for the study's existence and design; the "948 members" figure from a secondary source is unverified (LOW)
- Near-miss: Clark, Lawrence, Astley-Jones and Gray, *Neuron* 2009 (fMRI, slot-machine task). Near-misses were rated less pleasant than full misses yet increased the urge to keep playing, only when players felt in control, and recruited striatum and insula reward circuitry despite no monetary win. A 2010 follow-up (Chase and Clark, *J Neurosci*) found 20 regular gamblers' midbrain response to near-misses scaled with gambling severity. Press claims that this "releases dopamine" are interpretation; fMRI did not measure dopamine directly. — [PMC article PMC2929454](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2929454), [Society for Neuroscience 2010 summary](https://www.sfn.org/publications/latest-news/2010/05/04/problem-gamblers-provoked-by-near-misses-to-gamble-more), [Discover](https://discovermagazine.com/mind/slot-machine-near-misses-are-perfectly-tuned-to-stoke-the-addiction); HIGH for the main finding, with a small-sample caveat (reported as 15 volunteers by Discover; not confirmed in the paper)
- Variable-ratio reinforcement: reinforcement arrives after an unpredictable number of responses; textbook sources describe it as the schedule most productive and most resistant to extinction, with slot machines as the standard example. Application to loot boxes is stated as an analogy in these sources, not as an empirical loot-box study. — [Lumen Learning psychology text](https://content.one.lumenlearning.com/introductiontopsychology/chapter/reading-reinforcement-schedules/), [Psychology Today 2013](https://www.psychologytoday.com/gb/blog/brain-wise/201311/use-unpredictable-rewards-to-keep-behavior-going); MEDIUM
- Zeigarnik effect: Ghibellini and Meier (2025, *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*, DOI 10.1057/s41599-025-05000-w) found no memory advantage for unfinished tasks (recall ratio about 0.99) but a general tendency to resume interrupted tasks (about 67% resumption vs 50% baseline, the "Ovsiankina effect"); they conclude the Zeigarnik effect lacks universal validity. Study counts differ across summaries (59 vs 37). — [Palgrave article via RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/palcom/v12y2025i1d10.1057_s41599-025-05000-w.html), [Bern thesis](https://boristheses.unibe.ch/4796/), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeigarnik_effect); MEDIUM. Contrast with gamification blogs that present Zeigarnik as settled: [Yu-kai Chou page](https://yukaichou.com/behavioral-analysis/zeigarnik-effect-incomplete-tasks-memory-tension/).
- Loss aversion: contested. Gal and Rucker (*Journal of Consumer Psychology*, 2018) argued it is overstated; Mrkva et al. (2020, N = 17,720 across five samples) found people of all experience levels loss averse; a 2022 *Judgment and Decision Making* (17(5)) study that removed confounds found no loss aversion for small amounts and weak loss aversion at about $40 and mean lambda 1.54 at $100. — [JDM 2022 paper](https://dlab.sauder.ubc.ca/sjdm/journal/22/220414/jdm220414.html), [Mrkva et al. 2020 PDF](https://imo.unisg.ch/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/01_jcpy.1156.pdf); MEDIUM. The 2022 authors were not identified in the results.
- Scarcity/impatience, unpredictability, loss avoidance, ownership, social influence are named drives in Octalysis (see section 1), and "time-limited offers" and "direct exhortations to children" are specifically cited by EU consumer authorities as pressure tactics (see section 5). — [Forbrukertilsynet/CPC](https://www.forbrukertilsynet.no/eng-articles/national-consumer-authorities-and-commission-take-action-on-harmful-practices-in-the-gaming-industry), [CMS](https://cms.law/en/gbr/legal-updates/level-up-compliance-video-games-industry-in-the-spotlight-again-for-consumer-law-compliance); MEDIUM

### Inferences
- Endowed progress and goal gradient together explain progress bars, "1/10 complete" tutorials and pre-filled battle-pass tracks: both come from loyalty-card field data, not from games, so the transfer to games is plausible but not directly tested in the sources read.
- Because the Zeigarnik memory effect did not replicate but task resumption did, the safer claim for the report is "unfinished tasks create a pull to resume" (quest logs, incomplete sets), not "unfinished tasks are remembered better".
- Loss-aversion-based hooks (streak loss, expiring rewards) are probably still effective at the scale that matters to players (streaks feel large relative to a day's effort), but the 2022 study means "losses loom twice as large" should not be stated as settled law. This is my inference.

### Gaps
- Not sourced this run (background knowledge only, do not present as cited): sunk cost (Arkes and Blumer 1985), anchoring (Tversky and Kahneman 1974), reciprocity and commitment/consistency (Cialdini, *Influence*), social proof, original Kahneman-Tversky loss aversion, Skinner and Ferster's primary variable-ratio work. A report writer needing these should assign a follow-up search.
- No direct experiment found showing that any single mobile-game hook (daily login, streak, energy) raises retention causally; that evidence is likely in industry data covered by other researchers.
- No source found on whether gacha pity systems reduce or increase spending (relevant to "variable ratio" claims, since pity makes gacha partly fixed-ratio).

## 3. How do concrete features map to principles (daily login, streak, energy, gacha, battle pass, guild, starter pack, rewarded ad, limited event)?

### Takeaway
No source in this run provides a feature-by-feature psychology mapping from a peer-reviewed study. The table below is my synthesis: each principle is tied to a finding cited in section 2 or to an empirical dark-pattern study that names the feature.

### Cited Findings
- A 2026 study of 2024's top-grossing free-to-play games found that on mobile, daily rewards, opaque virtual currencies, loot boxes and socially driven incentives converge, and that several mirror gambling dynamics. — [Gutiérrez-Manjón et al., Tripodos 2026 (via Universidad de Valladolid portal)](https://portaldelaciencia.uva.es/documentos/6a209679ddc871d315e73a32); MEDIUM (abstract-level; link title says "taxonomy of dark patterns in top-grossing GaaS video games", may not be the same paper; authorship/year from the search summary)
- A heuristic evaluation of casual mobile games (2025) named Pay to Skip, Grinding and Playing by Appointment as key dark patterns. — [deceptive.design](https://www.deceptive.design/articles/finding-dark-patterns-in-casual-mobile-games-using-heuristic-evaluation); MEDIUM
- Zagal, Björk and Lewis (2013) examples: temporal patterns such as Grinding, monetary patterns such as Pay-to-Skip, and social patterns such as Social Pyramid Schemes. — [Chalmers record](https://research.chalmers.se/en/publication/177148), [arXiv 2401.06247 summary](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.06247); MEDIUM
- China's December 2023 draft rules proposed to ban daily login rewards and first-top-up / consecutive-recharge rewards, which shows regulators themselves treat these as spending/retention hooks. — [TechNode](https://technode.com/2023/12/22/draft-gaming-regulations-cause-sharp-fall-in-netease-and-tencent-share-prices/), [Techxplore](https://techxplore.com/news/2023-12-china-restrictions-online-gaming.amp); MEDIUM (see section 5 for status)

### Inferences (synthesis table, MEDIUM confidence; principle linkage is mine)
| Feature | Principles at work | Evidence anchor |
|---|---|---|
| Daily login reward | Habit trigger and prompt (Fogg, Hook); commitment build-up; goal gradient across a 7- or 30-day calendar; loss avoidance if a day is missed | Fogg B=MAP; Kivetz 2006; China 2023 draft targeted daily login rewards |
| Streak | Loss avoidance; sunk cost (not sourced); goal gradient; endowed progress once the streak is non-zero | Section 2 findings; loss-aversion caveat |
| Energy / stamina | Pacing that makes sessions short and frequent; return trigger when energy refills; "Playing by Appointment" dark pattern; sells a Pay-to-Skip | 2025 heuristic evaluation; Zagal 2013 |
| Gacha / loot box | Variable ratio; near-miss; unpredictability and curiosity; opaque virtual currency obscures cost | Clark 2009; Octalysis; FTC v. Cognosphere (section 5) |
| Battle pass | Goal gradient and endowed progress (tiers pre-filled/visible); sunk cost once purchased (not sourced); time-limited season creates scarcity | Nunes and Drèze 2006; Kivetz 2006 |
| Guild / clan | Social relatedness (SDT); obligation and reciprocity (not sourced); Bartle Socialisers | SDT 2006; Bartle 1996 |
| Starter pack | Anchoring and scarcity (limited-time, deep discount); first-purchase barrier removal; reciprocity (not sourced) | CPC cited time-limited offers as pressure tactic |
| Rewarded ad | Voluntary exchange (autonomy-preserving) in the best case; reciprocity; also supports Pay-to-Skip if used to bypass waits | No direct source |
| Limited-time event | Scarcity and impatience; fear of missing out; social proof via leaderboards | Octalysis; CPC "time-limited offers" |

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed source maps these features to principles one-to-one; the table is synthesized. Rewarded ads, guilds and starter packs have no empirical psychology source in this run.
- 2022-2023 cross-game dark-pattern studies were not found; the closest 2023 item is a single-game case study (Watcher of Realms, Moonton).

## 4. What is the evidence on harm and criticism (dark patterns, gambling-like mechanics, minors, spending)?

### Takeaway
There is a consistent small-to-moderate correlation (r about 0.25) between loot box spending and problem-gambling symptoms, but causation is not established; some longitudinal evidence for adolescents points to a gateway effect. Dark patterns are widespread across mobile games, including ones thought benign. Regulators treat children as especially vulnerable to obscured virtual-currency pricing.

### Cited Findings
- Meta-analysis (Garea et al. 2021): loot box spending correlates with problem-gambling symptoms at r = 0.26 (0.37 after trim-and-fill; 15 studies) and with excessive gaming at r = 0.25 (7 studies); another meta-analysis (Spicer et al. 2021) reports r = 0.27; a later review notes the 0.26 figure included unpublished studies. — [figshare record of the meta-analysis](https://figshare.utas.edu.au/articles/journal_contribution/Meta-analysis_of_the_relationship_between_problem_gambling_excessive_gaming_and_loot_box_spending/22996898/1/files/40746986.pdf), [McGill YGI Dec 2023 newsletter](https://www.youthgambling.mcgill.ca/Gambling2/en/PDF/YGI%20Newsletter%20Dec%202023.pdf); MEDIUM (figures read from search summary of those items)
- Correlation does not establish direction: it is unclear whether gambling-prone people spend more on loot boxes, or loot boxes cause harm, or a third factor explains both; spending and gambling data are self-reported. — same sources as above plus [Zendle and Cairns written evidence to UK Parliament](https://committees.parliament.uk/writtenevidence/98535/html/); MEDIUM
- Zendle et al. 2021 (*PLoS One* 16(4): e0247855), 726 collectible-card-game players: booster-pack spending showed no significant link to problem gambling in physical stores and only a trivial one online; cross-sectional design, Reddit sample skewed young and male, only 18 problem gamblers. Authors cite "r > .20" for loot boxes in contrast. — [PMC8023484](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8023484); HIGH
- Adolescents/young adults: a 2021 cross-sectional survey of 1,954 people aged 12-24 in NSW, Australia linked loot box purchasing to gambling problems, but found no evidence that longer loot box experience predicted current problems. A 2025 *BMC Psychology* longitudinal study reported baseline loot box spending predicted initiating conventional gambling at 6-month follow-up; a Spanish adolescent study reports similar results. — [CQU record](https://acquire.cqu.edu.au/articles/journal_contribution/Young_people_who_purchase_loot_boxes_are_more_likely_to_have_gambling_problems_An_online_survey_of_adolescents_and_young_adults_living_in_NSW_Australia/14984697), [BMC Psychology 2025](https://biomedcentral-bmcpsychology.publicaciones.saludcastillayleon.es/articles/10.1186/s40359-025-02766-1), [PMC8969855](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8969855); MEDIUM (abstract-level)
- UK government, July 2022: found a "stable and consistent" association between loot boxes and problem gambling but could not be sure of a causative link. — [Bird and Bird](https://mediawrites.twobirds.com/post/102j2z6/loot-boxes-uk-government-says-no-to-regulation), [Lewis Silkin](https://commercial.lewissilkin.com/post/102htqq/loot-boxes-to-avoid-regulation-following-uk-governments-response-to-call-for-evi); MEDIUM
- Australian government cited research that young people who played simulated gambling games are 40% more likely to spend real money on gambling as young adults (government claim reported by press). — [AGBrief 20 Sep 2024](https://agbrief.com/news/australia/20/09/2024/australia-tightens-regulations-on-loot-boxes-and-gambling-features-in-video-games/); LOW-MEDIUM
- Dark patterns are defined in Zagal, Björk and Lewis (2013, Foundations of Digital Games) as design elements where creator and player interests diverge; later work paraphrases them as patterns used intentionally to cause negative player experiences. — [Chalmers record](https://research.chalmers.se/en/publication/177148), [deceptive.design article](https://www.deceptive.design/articles/dark-patterns-in-the-design-of-games); MEDIUM
- Niknejad et al. (arXiv 2412.05039, Dec 2024) analyzed user-generated data on 1,496 iOS and Android games and found dark patterns are widespread, including in games perceived as benign. A DiGRA 2025 content analysis coded the top 50 US Google Play top-grossing F2P games (as of 16 June 2025). — [arXiv 2412.05039](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.05039), [deceptive.design summary](https://deceptive.design/articles/level-up-or-game-over-exploring-how-dark-patterns-shape-mobile-games), [DiGRA 2025 paper](https://dl.digra.org/index.php/dl/article/download/3101/3085/3144); MEDIUM
- Regulator view on children: FTC (Jan 2025) alleged Cognosphere unfairly sold bundled, multi-tiered virtual currency and loot boxes to children and teens who have a harder time judging the cost, and that currency-only purchase obscured real spending. — [FTC business blog 17 Jan 2025](https://ftc.gov/business-guidance/blog/2025/01/level-tips-businesses-ftcs-settlement-genshin-impact-developer-hoyoverse); HIGH
- FTC dark-pattern case: Epic Games (Fortnite) agreed in December 2022 to pay $245 million in consumer refunds for "dark patterns" (confusing button configuration leading to unwanted charges; locking accounts of players who disputed charges) plus $275 million for COPPA violations, $520 million in total; the refund order was finalized in March 2023. — [FTC node 80648](https://www.ftc.gov/node/80648), [KSAT 19 Dec 2022](https://www.ksat.com/business/2022/12/19/fortnite-maker-epic-games-to-pay-520m-to-settle-ftc-cases/), [PYMNTS](https://www.pymnts.com/news/regulation/2023/ftc-says-epic-games-set-to-refund-245-million-to-fortnite-players/); HIGH for the amounts; the 2024 refund details (about $114 average) come from low-reliability sources and are LOW
- Korea: the Korea Fair Trade Commission fined Nexon Korea 11.6 billion won (about $8.9 million) in about January 2024 for lowering gacha drop rates without telling players (including cases of 0%), the largest fine ever under the e-commerce consumer protection act; Nexon said disclosure was not required at the time. — [Siliconera](https://www.siliconera.com/nexon-fined-over-8-million-for-manipulating-item-drop-rates-in-games/), [Massively OP](https://massivelyop.com/?p=161909), [PCGamesN](https://www.pcgamesn.com/the-finals/nexon-fine); MEDIUM (press; exact date not confirmed)

### Inferences
- The honest summary for a lay reader: loot box spending and gambling problems travel together, the strength is small to moderate, and whether one causes the other is unresolved; adolescent longitudinal studies lean toward a possible gateway effect. This is a hedged reading of mixed evidence.
- The cases that produced real penalties (Epic, Cognosphere, Nexon) were about deception (odds, drop rates, confusing buy buttons) and children, not about variable-ratio rewards as such. The practical enforcement line so far is deception plus minors.

### Gaps
- No source on gacha-specific harm to minors (search returned loot-box literature, not gacha-titled studies).
- Mobile-specific spending concentration (for example whale share of revenue) is outside this slice and covered by other researchers.
- WHO ICD-11 "gaming disorder" and addiction prevalence were not researched in this run.
- 5Rights (advocacy group) reports "widespread" non-compliance with UK self-regulatory loot box principles; publication date not confirmed (see section 5).

## 5. What is the regulation and policy picture as of October 2026?

### Takeaway
The regime is a patchwork. Hard bans on paid loot boxes exist or are being enforced for minors in Belgium and Brazil; odds disclosure is the baseline in China, South Korea, Apple and Google rules; the US acts through FTC deception/unfairness and COPPA cases; the UK relies on self-regulation; the EU is moving fast on virtual currency and minors (CPC action 15 Oct 2026, Digital Fairness Act proposal reportedly due November 2026).

### Cited Findings

EU (most dynamic, newest developments)
- Digital Fairness Act (DFA) timeline: Fitness Check published October 2024; public consultation ran 17 July to 24 October 2025; Commission 2026 Work Programme planned a proposal for Q4 2026; as of Heuking's article (dated "08-05-2026", ambiguous between 8 May and 5 August 2026) no concrete draft existed. — [Heuking](https://www.heuking.de/en/news-events/newsletter-articles/detail/digital-fairness-act-implementation-obligations-companies-need-to-prepare-for.html); MEDIUM
- Latest: Lewis Silkin (15 Oct 2026) says the Commission has reportedly said it intends to publish its draft DFA in November (year not stated; context implies November 2026) and expects it to cover dark patterns, addictive design, unfair personalisation, influencer marketing, loot boxes and virtual currencies. As of 22 Oct 2026 no proposal text was found. — [Lewis Silkin 15 Oct 2026](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2026/10/15/eu-consumer-protection-network-launches-action-involving-nine-video-games-compani-102o5do); MEDIUM
- Earlier timing statements conflict: Q3 2026 (Lewis Silkin, April 2025), "before end of 2026" (PL&B), Q4 2026 (Heuking). — [Lewis Silkin Apr 2025](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2025/04/09/the-consumer-law-revolution-continues-in-the-eu-102k84o), [PL&B](https://www.privacylaws.com/news/eu-proposal-on-digital-fairness-act-expected-by-the-end-of-2026/); MEDIUM. Contradiction: the timeline slipped, so the Nov 2026 date is itself unconfirmed.
- DFA consultation content: the consultation did not call for outright loot box bans but pushed for scrutiny of defaults, opt-ins and child-focused design; respondents wanted clearer odds disclosure and real-money prices; about 70% wanted new binding rules on in-game spending. Response counts conflict: Chambers says over 3,300 consultation responses plus 4,300+ call-for-evidence submissions; Heuking says 4,324 valid responses. — [Chambers 3 Feb 2026](https://chambers.com/articles/digital-fairness-act-what-the-public-consultation-tells-the-video-game-industry), [Heuking](https://www.heuking.de/en/news-events/newsletter-articles/detail/digital-fairness-act-implementation-obligations-companies-need-to-prepare-for.html); MEDIUM

## 6. Where does engaging design end and exploitative design begin, and what do practitioners recommend?

### Takeaway
No consensus line exists in the literature, but three tests recur: (1) does the design hide cost or odds (deception), (2) is it aimed at children or vulnerable players, and (3) does it serve the player's goals or only the creator's (Zagal's divergent-interest test). Regulators and enforcers have so far drawn the line at deception plus minors rather than at engagement mechanics themselves. Practitioner-written ethical guidance is thin in this run.

### Cited Findings
- Zagal, Björk and Lewis (2013) frame dark patterns as game design elements that serve the creator's interest against the player's, and offer guiding questions for identifying them. — [Chalmers record](https://research.chalmers.se/en/publication/177148), [Gothenburg repository PDF](https://gup.ub.gu.se/file/101018); MEDIUM-HIGH
- A DiGRA 2020 paper frames the ethical debate as whether players are making free, informed purchase and play decisions or are compelled, deceived or "addicted". — [White Rose eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/156460/1); MEDIUM
- SDT-based distinction: "harmonious" passion arises under high need satisfaction and "obsessive" passion under low need satisfaction (hypothesis from Przybylski et al. 2009), giving a psychological definition of engagement the player would endorse vs compulsion. — [Ewha record](https://pure.ewha.ac.kr/en/publications/having-to-versus-wanting-to-play-background-and-consequences-of-h/); MEDIUM (hypothesis only; results not read)
- Regulator-defined floor from enforcement orders: truthful odds and cost disclosure; a direct real-money purchase path; parental consent for loot boxes for under-16s (FTC order terms); and CPC principles on clear pricing, no hidden costs, no forced currency purchase and protection of children. — [FTC blog](https://ftc.gov/business-guidance/blog/2025/01/level-tips-businesses-ftcs-settlement-genshin-impact-developer-hoyoverse), [Forbrukertilsynet](https://www.forbrukertilsynet.no/eng-articles/national-consumer-authorities-and-commission-take-action-on-harmful-practices-in-the-gaming-industry); HIGH
- Practitioner suggestions in a design blog: be upfront about in-app purchase costs and probabilities and avoid artificial scarcity or pressuring players to spend. — [Wayline](https://www.wayline.io/blog/predatory-monetization-mobile-gaming); LOW (low-authority blog)
- Academic recommendation: an arXiv study of 1,496 games advocates community-based approaches, with player and practitioner collaboration to surface harmful design. — [arXiv 2412.05039](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.05039); MEDIUM
- A qualitative case study of Moonton's *Watcher of Realms* (2023) found player interpretations range from normalizing the monetization model to explicit critique, spending rationalization and self-regulation strategies. — [DiGRA / Universidad de Valladolid portal listing](https://portaldelaciencia.uva.es/documentos/6a17f944422e1448b6743906); LOW-MEDIUM (listing title is "Play, Spend, Eat Sand: How Gacha Design and Player Communities Normalize Spending", which I assume is the same study; the link between title and the Watcher of Realms description is inferred)
- Industry counter-position: Video Games Europe and the European Game Developers Federation reject treating in-game currencies as digital representations of value, and the industry opposes mandatory real-money price display as creating artificial benchmarks. — [Lewis Silkin 15 Oct 2026](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2026/10/15/eu-consumer-protection-network-launches-action-involving-nine-video-games-compani-102o5do), [Heuking](https://www.heuking.de/en/news-events/newsletter-articles/detail/digital-fairness-act-implementation-obligations-companies-need-to-prepare-for.html); MEDIUM

### Inferences
- A usable three-question test for the report: (a) Would the player, shown exactly how the mechanic works and what it costs, still choose it (informed consent)? (b) Does it work by creating anxiety, shortage or obligation rather than by delivering competence, relatedness or enjoyment (SDT lens)? (c) Is the audience likely to include children, who regulators treat as less able to judge cost? This synthesizes Zagal, SDT and the FTC/CPC reasoning; it is not a published standard.
- Mechanics regulators have actually challenged (paid randomized rewards sold to minors, currency bundles that obscure price, time-limited pressure aimed at children) sit at the exploitative end; progress bars, social play and mastery loops are largely uncontested in the sources found. This is a reading of enforcement patterns, not a stated rule.

### Gaps
- No practitioner code of conduct was retrieved: the Fair Play Alliance "Healthy Game Design" material, Deconstructor of Fun, GDC talks on ethical monetization and Nir Eyal's own ethics tests (for example the "regret test" in *Indistractable*) were not found or fetched. The only practitioner-sourced guidance here is a low-authority blog.
- No evidence on whether ethical-design retrofits (odds display, spend caps, cool-down warnings) reduce harm or revenue; no controlled study was found.
- The DiGRA 2020 paper and the Zagal paper bodies were not read in full, so their detailed guiding questions are not reproduced.
